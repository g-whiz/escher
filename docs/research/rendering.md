## Abstract

This document collects the published algorithms for drawing a tiling of the sphere, the Euclidean plane, or the hyperbolic plane, generated by a discrete group of isometries, into a raster image. It covers the per-pixel pull-back of each pixel into a fundamental domain, the forward generation of tiles, the drawing of tile borders, antialiasing, the sRGB encoding of 8-bit PNG output, the maps from other models of the hyperbolic plane and from the sphere to the model in which the fold runs, pattern fills, and CPU performance. For each method it gives the formulas, the loop structure, the constants used in published code, the numerical caveats, and the conventions on which sources disagree, so that an implementer can choose one convention and code it.

## 1. Scope

This document covers the algorithms that turn a group, given by a fundamental domain and its side transforms, into pixels. It states formulas, loops, constants, and caveats as the cited sources give them.

It leaves these topics to other documents and to its sources:

- The models of the hyperbolic plane, their isometries, and the construction of a group from an orbifold signature belong to `docs/research/geometry-models.md`. Section 8 of this document gives only the maps a renderer applies to a pixel before folding.
- The SymmHub source code is described in depth in `docs/research/symmhub.md`. This document cites only the SymmHub facts that rendering needs.
- Phong shading of the sphere, animation, and vector export are out of scope. The research behind this document found no source on Phong shading of a tiled sphere.
- Sources that could not be read in full are listed in Section 13.2 and recorded in `docs/research/source-requests.md`.

## 2. Notation and Conventions

### 2.1. Symbols

- $F$: the fundamental domain of the group, bounded by its sides.
- $v$, $z$: the point being folded, as a vector or as a complex number.
- $c$, $r$: the centre and radius of a mirror circle or sphere.
- $n$, $d$: the unit normal and offset of a mirror line or plane.
- $s$: the scale factor tracked through the fold, the local ratio of lengths at the folded point to lengths at the pixel.
- $\langle x, y \rangle$: the Lorentzian inner product on the hyperboloid model. Section 4.3 gives the signature that each source uses.
- $\ell(w)$: the length of a group element $w$ as a word in the generators.
- $R$, $\rho$: the Euclidean radius of a point in the Poincare disk and its hyperbolic distance from the centre.
- $\varphi$: the polar angle of a point.
- $k$: the order of a dihedral corner in Stampfli's method.
- $p$, $q$: the parameters of the regular tessellation $\{p, q\}$, in which $q$ regular $p$-gons meet at each vertex.
- $x \circ y$: Ratcliffe's Lorentzian inner product. In Chapter 3 of [RATCLIFFE] it is $x \circ y = -x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2 + \dots + x_n y_n$, with the time coordinate first ([RATCLIFFE], first edition, Section 3.1, equation (3.1.1), p. 56).
- $\lVert x \rVert$, $\lvert\lvert\lvert y \rvert\rvert\rvert$: Ratcliffe's Lorentzian norm of a space-like vector $x$ and of a time-like vector $y$ ([RATCLIFFE], first edition, Section 3.2).
- $\eta(x, y)$: the hyperbolic distance of Ratcliffe's Theorem 3.2.12, which Section 6.2 states.
- $C_{\mathrm{lin}}$, $C'$: a linear-light colour component and its sRGB-encoded value, in Section 7.5.
- $I$, $b$: an integer sample of a PNG image and its sample depth in bits, in Section 7.5.

A table or a formula that follows a source keeps that source's letters and defines them there. For example, $p$ and $q$ denote points in Sections 4.3 and 6.2, $R$, $\rho$, $S$, and $W$ take other meanings in Section 4.7, and $\rho$ is Beardon's hyperbolic distance in Section 6.2.

### 2.2. Terms

- fundamental domain: a region $F$ whose images under the group tile the space without overlap.
- side pairing: the assignment to each side of $F$ of the group element that maps $F$ to the neighbouring tile across that side.
- fold: one application of a side transform to the point being pulled back. The fold loop repeats folds until the point lies in $F$.
- per-pixel pull-back: rendering by mapping each pixel into $F$ with a fold loop and colouring it from its position there. Sources also call it folding, reverse pixel lookup, or the inverse mapping technique.
- forward generation: rendering by enumerating the tiles, or the group elements, and drawing each tile as a polygon.
- splane: SymmHub's name for a generalized sphere, which is a circle or a line in the plane [SYMMHUB].
- iteration cap: the maximum number of passes of the fold loop.
- limit circle: the boundary circle of the Poincare disk, where tiles shrink to zero Euclidean size.
- exposure: in Dunham's algorithm, the number of edges that a polygon of one layer shares with the next layer [DUNHAM07].
- signed side test: a function of a point and a side of $F$ whose sign says on which side of the mirror the point lies.
- linear-light component: a colour component before the sRGB transfer function encodes it. The term is that of [CSS-COLOUR-4].
- encoded component: a colour component after the sRGB transfer function, which is what an sRGB sample stores.
- sRGB transfer function: the nonlinear map between a linear-light component and an encoded component, in both directions.
- rendering intent: the one-byte field of the PNG sRGB chunk that names the ICC rendering intent with which the image should be displayed [PNG3]. ICC is the consortium that [ICC-SRGB] names.

Names with accented letters are written in ASCII transliteration, for example Poincare, Cheritat, Kopczynski, Celinska-Kopczynska, Svarc, and Mobius. A title, a name, or a URL that contains a spelling that the prose checker rejects, such as the American `color`, is set in a code span.

Page numbers for [DAVIS] are the printed pages, which are 16 less than the page numbers of its PDF file. Page numbers for [VGRG] are the same in print and in the PDF file. SymmHub paths are relative to the root of the SymmHub repository at commit f2d19cb. All URLs in Section 14 were accessed on 2026-11-15, except those of the sources of Section 7.5, which were accessed on 2026-11-16.

A claim that rests only on a secondary source, such as Wikipedia, a blog, a search snippet, or a summary by a fetch tool, carries the mark "(secondary source only)". A claim with no such mark was checked against a primary source: a paper read in full, the source code, or a report on the SymmHub source.

## 3. The Two Rendering Families

Per-pixel pull-back maps each pixel to a model point and applies side transforms of $F$ until the point lies in $F$. The final position gives the colour, the border distance, and the motif lookup. The method is the standard raster method in all three geometries, and SymmHub uses one loop for all of them [SYMMHUB]. Bulatov states that the work is "independent for each point... easily parallelized" ([BULATOV14], p. 330).

Forward generation enumerates the tiles once each and then draws or texture-maps polygons. Dunham's goal is "a replication algorithm that would draw each copy of a motif exactly once" [DUNHAM03]. Forward generation suits a list of tiles, such as arc objects in a vector plotter ([HYPERTILING], Section 3.6, secondary source only).

Bulatov rejects forward enumeration for raster output, because "the count of needed transformations grows very rapidly with the size of tiles approaching zero" ([BULATOV14], p. 330). Cheritat lists the trade-offs of the per-pixel method as "max iterations (almost) not a problem", "aliasing near the boundary", "more computation (slower)", "easy to program", and "easy to adapt" ([CHERITAT], secondary source only).

## 4. Per-Pixel Pull-Back

### 4.1. The Fold Loop

SymmHub folds a point with this loop, which the GPU function `iToFundamentalDomainSampler` implements ([SYMMHUB], `lib/shaders/inversiveSampler.glsl.mjs:182-236`):

```glsl
for(int count = 0; count < iterations; count++){
  int found = 0;
  for(int g = 0; g < domainSize; g++){
    iSPlane sp = fetchSplane(groupData, domainSplanesOffset + g*2);
    float ip = iDistance(sp, pnt);
    if(ip > EPS){ /* for r < refCount: iReflect(rsp, pnt, scale); */ refcount++; found = 1; break; }
  }
  if(found == 0){ inDomain = 1; break; }
}
```

In steps, the loop does this:

1. Map the pixel to a model point $v$ and set the scale $s$ from the view chain (Section 8.2).
2. For each side of $F$ in order, evaluate the signed side test (Section 4.3).
3. If no side has a test value above `EPS`, mark the point as inside $F$ and stop.
4. Otherwise take the first violated side and apply its transform, a list of reflections, to $v$. Each reflection also updates $s$ and the colour state (Section 4.6).
5. Repeat from step 2 until the iteration cap is reached. A point that reaches the cap stays outside $F$ (Section 4.9).

These constants and variants apply:

- `EPS = 1.e-7` ([SYMMHUB], `lib/shaders/inversiveSampler.glsl.mjs:184`). A snap of near-zero test values to zero, `//if(abs(ip) < EPS) ip = 0.0;`, is commented out at line 208.
- `uIterations` defaults to 10, with a range of 0 to 1000 in the user interface ([SYMMHUB], `lib/symhublib/SymRenderer.js:393`). The colour preset for the group 442 uses 40.
- The CPU version `toFundDomain` has a default `maxIterations = 20`, no tolerance, and assumes a convex domain ([SYMMHUB], `lib/invlib/Group.js:191`).

Bulatov takes the per-pixel method from the "inverse mapping technique used [4] for 2D hyperbolic geometry", where [4] is von Gagern and Richter-Gebert ([BULATOV14], p. 330). They call it reverse pixel lookup. Its principle is that "every pixel of the disk belongs to exactly one such tile (neglecting pixels on boundaries), and every tile can be mapped onto a central tile" with known colours "by a finite number of semigroup generator transformations" ([VGRG], Section 4.2, p. 22). They colour pixels "moving from the center outwards". This block quotes their four steps for each pixel verbatim, in the source's spelling ([VGRG], Section 4.2, p. 22, Figure 13):

```text
1. First apply the transformation (green arrow) which mapped its neighboring
   pixel (green circle, 2) into the central tile. The result (red circle, 3)
   is usually at least quite close to the central tile.
2. While the transformed position is still outside of any boundary line of
   the central tile (blue line), apply the generator associated with this
   boundary line (blue arrow). If the result (red circle, 4) still isn't
   inside the central tile, repeat this step until it finally is.
3. Round to integral pixel coordinates.
4. Copy pixel color.
```

Step 1 is a published optimization. Because "For most of the area, two adjacent pixels will belong to the same tile", the transformation of a neighbouring pixel is a good starting point, so the loop of step 2 starts close to the central tile ([VGRG], p. 22). The method implies one retained transformation per pixel, but the paper does not say how it is stored, as a matrix, a Mobius transformation, or a word. Step 3 is plain rounding to integer pixel coordinates, which is nearest-pixel sampling of a raster of the central tile.

Section 4.2 of [VGRG] leaves these points unstated:

- Any proof, bound, or count of the iterations, beyond "a finite number" and "repeat this step until it finally is".
- Floating-point precision, antialiasing, and the handling of points on a boundary, beyond "(neglecting pixels on boundaries)".
- The order in which several violated boundary lines are tested.
- Whether the generators are reflections, and whether the central tile is a triangle or a general polygon.
- The order of the pixels beyond "moving from the center outwards".

The paper's Section 6.2 says that the method should also work for spherical geometry, with no detail ([VGRG], p. 27).

### 4.2. Reflection Groups and General Side Pairings

Davis gives the condition under which a polytope is the fundamental domain of a reflection group. Let $P^n$ be a simple convex polytope in $\mathbb{S}^n$, $\mathbb{E}^n$, or $\mathbb{H}^n$ with $n \ge 2$. Let its dihedral angles be $\pi/m_{ij}$ for integers $m_{ij}$ wherever the faces $F_i$ and $F_j$ meet, and set $m_{ij} = \infty$ where they do not. Then the group generated by the reflections in its faces is isomorphic to the Coxeter group of the matrix $(m_{ij})$, acts properly, and has $P^n$ as a strict fundamental domain ([DAVIS], Theorem 6.4.3, p. 83). A strict fundamental domain meets each orbit in exactly one point ([DAVIS], Definition 5.1.3, p. 64). The group is discrete and the space is tiled by congruent copies of $P^n$ ([DAVIS], Remark 6.4.5, p. 84).

For $n = 2$, a hyperbolic $m$-gon with angles $\pi/m_i$ and $\sum_{i=1}^m 1/m_i < m - 2$ has a convex realization and so gives a reflection group ([DAVIS], Example 6.5.3, in Section 6.5, pp. 85-87). Davis defines a Euclidean convex polytope as compact and a hyperbolic one through a cone inside the light cone. Chapter 6 does not treat ideal vertices ([DAVIS], pp. 74-76). Epstein and Petronio, and the first edition of Ratcliffe, state the polygon theorem for domains with ideal vertices, with its hypotheses [EPSTEIN-PETRONIO1994] [RATCLIFFE]. Section 4.7 of `docs/research/fundamental-domains.md` covers the theorem, and this document does not restate it. Neither source states how a fold behaves at an ideal vertex (Section 13.1, item 3).

For a pure reflection group, each side's transform is the single reflection in that side, which `GroupUtils.makeReflections` builds ([SYMMHUB], `lib/invlib/Group.js:57-62`). For a general side-pairing group, each side's transform is the pairing element written as a list of reflections, so the same loop handles both [SYMMHUB].

The header of `lib/grouplib/Group_ON.js` states that the fold "still converges since every transform is a group element", for a group in which one side appears twice in the side list [SYMMHUB]. The bounded-domain variant assumes a convex domain ([SYMMHUB], `lib/invlib/Inversive.js:1249`). Termination without convexity is open (Section 13.1, item 7).

Cheritat gives a reduction to a Dirichlet domain $D$ about a base point $M$ for a general group with generators $K$: "if for some g in K, dist(g.P,M) < dist(P,M): P = g.P", then continue, else exit ([CHERITAT], secondary source only, transliterated). The slides state that "the Dirichlet domain D is a fundamental domain" and give the method for $\mathbb{H}^3$. Its use for Fuchsian groups with $K$ the side pairings is an inference of the research (Section 13.1, item 7).

For finite linear reflection groups only, Davis shows that the fundamental chamber $C$ is the Dirichlet domain about a point $x_0$ in its interior, because $d(sx, x_0) \ge d(x, x_0)$ and, by induction on $\ell(w)$, $d(wx, x_0) \ge d(x, x_0)$ ([DAVIS], Complement 6.6.10, pp. 91-92). He adds that "there is no reason for" a Dirichlet domain "to be a strict fundamental domain" (same place).

Stampfli gives a fold for a triangle with one arc side and a dihedral corner of order $k$ at the centre ([STAMPFLI], pp. 67-72):

1. Reduce the angle to the sector: $\varphi \leftarrow \varphi - \frac{360^\circ}{k}\left\lfloor \varphi \Big/ \frac{360^\circ}{k} \right\rfloor$. Then, if $\varphi > 180^\circ/k$, set $\varphi \leftarrow 360^\circ/k - \varphi$ (p. 67). Use "the universal floor function and not the modulo function, which might give strange results for negative" angles (p. 67).
2. Test the point against the circle of the arc. In the hyperbolic case, invert the point in that circle (Section 4.3) if it lies inside (p. 70). In the spherical case, invert it if it lies outside (pp. 70-71), and Stampfli notes that there is "no limiting circle" in this case. After an inversion, another iteration starts.

For a regular $k$-gon, step 2 picks instead the arc whose sector $i\frac{360^\circ}{k} < \varphi < (i+1)\frac{360^\circ}{k}$ contains the point. Its circle is centred at polar angle $(i+\tfrac12)\frac{360^\circ}{k}$. Invert if the point is inside, otherwise stop (p. 72). Stampfli suggests speeding up the trigonometric functions "using interpolated function tables" (p. 68).

### 4.3. Primitives

SymmHub represents every geometry as splanes in one plane. The disk is the hyperbolic case, the stereographic plane the spherical case, and the Euclidean plane the flat case [SYMMHUB]. Bulatov calls inversive geometry "a universal container for all three classic geometries", with dihedral angles that are "submultiples of $\pi$ for intersecting splanes or 0 for tangent splanes" ([BULATOV14], p. 329).

SymmHub stores a splane as a four-component vector. For a sphere, the first three components are the centre $c$ and the fourth is the signed radius $r$, as in `iSphere([cx, cy, 0, r])`. For a plane, the code stores the unit normal $n$ in the field `.center` and the offset $d$ in the field `.radius`, as in `iPlane([nx, ny, 0, d])` ([SYMMHUB], `lib/shaders/isplane.glsl.mjs:26-45`). In `iDistance`, a positive radius makes the inside of the circle negative, and a negative radius makes the outside negative. $F$ is the set where every side test is at most zero, so a side with a positive radius keeps $F$ inside its circle and a side with a negative radius keeps $F$ outside it ([SYMMHUB], `lib/shaders/isplane.glsl.mjs:26-45`). Section 2.3 lists the other sign conventions.

SymmHub builds the fundamental triangle of the group with corner angles $a_0$, $a_1$, $a_2$ in `makeTriangle` ([SYMMHUB], `lib/grouplib/Group_KLM.js:292-312`). It uses the ideal construction when $\lvert a_0 \rvert < 10^{-6}$. Otherwise, with $\Sigma = a_0 + a_1 + a_2$, it uses the Euclidean construction when $\lvert \Sigma - \pi \rvert < 10^{-6}$, the spherical one when $\Sigma > \pi + 10^{-6}$, and the hyperbolic one otherwise. The angle of a corner of order $K$ is $\pi/K$, and $K$ of 0, 1, or at least 20 gives the angle 0, an ideal corner (`getAngle`, lines 318-324).

In the three non-ideal constructions, the first two sides are the lines `iPlane([0., -1., 0., 0.])` and `iPlane([-sin(a0), cos(a0), 0., 0.])`, which meet at the origin. The third side is this ([SYMMHUB], `lib/grouplib/Group_KLM.js`):

| Geometry | Third side | Constants | Lines |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Euclidean | `iPlane([sin(a1), cos(a1), 0., e])`, the line $x\sin a_1 + y\cos a_1 = e$ | $e$ is `triSize`, default 0.25 | 355-366 |
| Spherical | `iSphere([cx, cy, 0, -r])` | $r = \dfrac{e_2 \sin a_0}{\cos a_2 + \cos(a_1 + a_0)}$, $c_x = r\,\dfrac{\cos a_2 + \cos a_1 \cos a_0}{\sin a_0}$, $c_y = r\cos a_1$, with $e_2$ the parameter `triSize`, default 0.25 | 332-349 |
| Hyperbolic | `iSphere([cx, cy, 0, -r])` | $a_x = \dfrac{\cos a_2 + \cos a_1 \cos a_0}{\sin a_0}$, $a_y = \cos a_1$, $r = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{a_x^2 + a_y^2 - 1}}$, $c_x = r a_x$, $c_y = r a_y$ | 371-389 |

In the hyperbolic row $r > 0$, so the radius stored is negative and $F$ lies outside the third circle. No caller passes `triSize`, so the default applies.

The ideal construction (lines 398-436) uses the line `iPlane([0., -1., 0., 0.])` and the circle `iSphere([-1., 1., 0., -1.])` as its first two sides. It builds the third side as the circle with centre $(1, r_3\cos a_1)$ and radius $r_3 = 2/(\cos a_1 + \cos a_2)$, and then inverts it in the circle with centre $(-1, 0)$ and radius 2. In the code, with $x_3 = 1$ and $y_3 = r_3\cos a_1$:

$$d_d = (x_3 + 1)^2 + y_3^2 - r_3^2, \qquad a = \frac{4}{d_d}, \qquad x_3' = -1 + a(x_3 + 1), \qquad y_3' = a y_3, \qquad r_3' = a r_3$$

and the third side is `iSphere([x3, y3, 0, -r3])` with the new values. These special cases apply:

- When $\lvert \cos a_1 + \cos a_2 \rvert < 10^{-6}$, the third side is the unit circle `iSphere([0., 0., 0., 1.])`.
- When $\lvert d_d \rvert \le 10^{-4}$, the third side is the line `iPlane([1., 0., 0, 0.])` if $\lvert a_2 \rvert < 10^{-6}$, and the line `iPlane([1., 1., 0, 0.])` otherwise.

### 4.6. Colouring State

### 4.7. Termination and Growth of the Fold Count

Davis states the length results that bear on termination for group elements and chambers. In these statements $W$ is a Coxeter group with the set $S$ of fundamental reflections, and $R(u, v)$ is the set of reflections whose wall separates $u$ from $v$ in the Cayley graph $\mathrm{Cay}(W, S)$ ([DAVIS], p. 46):

- $\ell(u) = \mathrm{Card}(R(1, u))$, and $d(u, v) = \mathrm{Card}(R(u, v))$ for the word metric $d$. Also $R(1, uv)$ is the symmetric difference of $R(1, u)$ and $R(u, uv)$ ([DAVIS], Lemma 4.2.1, p. 46).
- "Given a reflection r in R and an element w in W, r in R(1,w) if and only if l(w) > l(rw)." ([DAVIS], Lemma 4.2.2, p. 46, transliterated). The lemma gives a strict decrease for any reflection, not a decrease by exactly one.
- For a fundamental reflection $s \in S$, $\ell(sw) = \ell(w) \pm 1$, because the Exchange Condition excludes $\ell(sw) = \ell(w)$ ([DAVIS], Remark on pp. 35-36).
- In the geometric representation, "the chambers C and wC lie on opposite sides of the hyperplane" of $s$ "if and only if l(w) > l(sw)" ([DAVIS], Lemma D.2.2, p. 443, transliterated). Here $C$ is the fundamental simplicial cone in the dual space of the canonical representation.
- For finite linear groups, if $x, y \in C$ and $wx = y$, then $x = y$ and $w$ lies in the stabilizer subgroup $W_{S(x)}$ ([DAVIS], Lemma 6.6.8, p. 90). Its proof is by induction on $\ell(w)$ and is the closest the book comes to bringing a point into the chamber.

Davis does not state any algorithm that reflects a point until it lies in the chamber, and gives no bound on the number of reflection steps, in $\ell(w)$, in the hyperbolic distance of the point, or otherwise. He does not state, for a point of a hyperbolic polytope group, that the number of walls separating it from the fundamental chamber equals $\ell(w)$. The "Folding Condition" (F) of his Section 3.2 is a length condition on group elements, not the point fold. For hyperbolic groups the strict fundamental domain comes from Theorem 6.4.3 by a covering argument, not by induction on length. A reading of Chapters 1, 3 to 6, and 17 and Appendix D of [DAVIS] found none of the missing statements.

These results from Casselman's notes also bear on termination:

- "The length of w is the number of root hyperplans [sic] separating C from wC." ([CASSELMAN-1], corollary after Proposition 20).
- "The root hyperplanes for r in Lw are those separating C from w^-1 C." ([CASSELMAN-1], Proposition 19, transliterated).
- "sw > w if and only if wC lies entirely in the region as > 0." ([CASSELMAN-1], Theorem 15).
- Proposition 26 characterizes the Tits cone ([CASSELMAN-1]).
- The reduction loop for a word: "let s be such that wa is negative and calculate ws", then "Repeat until there are no negative coordinates" ([CASSELMAN-2], section "Vectors").
- A reduction procedure with a termination proof for the example of $PSL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ is in Section 1, Proposition 1.1, of [CASSELMAN-A].

No source states that folding a point takes exactly $\ell(w)$ steps for any choice of violated wall (Section 13.1, item 1).

The first edition of [RATCLIFFE], [BEARDON], and [EPSTEIN-PETRONIO1994] were searched for a procedure that moves a point into a fundamental domain, for its termination, and for a count of its steps, and none of them states one. Epstein and Petronio note that Riley's programs improve a guess for a fundamental domain, and that this feature "is not addressed in this paper" ([EPSTEIN-PETRONIO1994], introduction, pp. 113-114).

The number of folds has no upper bound in a hyperbolic group. Nelson and Segerman state "there is no upper bound on the number of reflections needed to move the location of a pixel" ([NS], Appendix A.1).

Davis defines the growth series $f(t) = \sum_{g \in G} t^{\ell(g)} = \sum_n a_n t^n$, where $a_n$ is the number of elements of word length $n$ ([DAVIS], Chapter 17, p. 315). For a Coxeter group it is a rational function $f(t)/g(t)$ with integer polynomials ([DAVIS], Corollary 17.1.6, p. 318), and its radius of convergence $\rho$ is the smallest positive root of $g$ ([DAVIS], p. 322). For an infinite Coxeter system, $\rho = 1$ holds exactly when the group has subexponential growth, and exactly when it decomposes as the product of a finite group and a cocompact Euclidean reflection group ([DAVIS], Proposition 17.2.1, p. 323). For the right-angled pentagon group, $\rho = (3 - \sqrt5)/2$ ([DAVIS], Example 17.1.15, p. 322).

Davis gives no explicit growth rate for hyperbolic triangle groups and no count of chambers within a hyperbolic distance. Wikipedia states that hyperbolic triangle groups have exponential growth ([WP-GROWTH], secondary source only).

Stampfli gives the per-pixel cost: "An iteration step roughly moves a point by one unit cell of the pattern towards the center... most pixel positions require only a few iterations" ([STAMPFLI], p. 70). The fold count is therefore roughly proportional to the hyperbolic distance from $F$. The disk radius and the hyperbolic distance from the centre are related by ([CFKP], Fact 5, pp. 92-93):

$$\rho = \ln\frac{1+R}{1-R}, \qquad R = \frac{e^\rho - 1}{e^\rho + 1}$$

How the fold count grows with $\lvert z \rvert$ is open (Section 13.1, item 5).

### 4.8. Precision

Points near the limit circle lose precision. Kopczynski and Celinska-Kopczynska state: "In the Klein disk model, a point in distance d from C0 is mapped to a point in distance tanh(d) from the center of the disk, which is $1 - \Theta(\exp(2d))$. Floating point numbers cannot express such a slight difference from 1. Conversely, in the Poincare disk model, this is $1 - \Theta(\exp(d))$." ([KOPCZ24], Section 6, p. 9). Section 2.3 gives the reading of the exponent signs.

The same paper gives these countermeasures:

- "In the half-plane and half-space models, we no longer have the problem of faraway points getting close to 1." ([KOPCZ24], p. 11).
- The polar representation is best, "although the halfplane invariant is also very successful" ([KOPCZ24], Section 8).
- Points are stored relative to their tile, as a pair $(t, p_0)$ ([KOPCZ24], Section 4, p. 8).
- HyperRogue re-orthogonalizes its matrices, because otherwise the view becomes "visibly stretched" ([KOPCZ24], Section 3, p. 7).

SymmHub applies these measures [SYMMHUB]:

- The view anchor pre-folds the view with a group element computed in double precision on the CPU, so that only the small offset is evaluated in single precision on the GPU. Without it, "a view far from F runs out of steps and stops drawing" (`lib/grouplib/ViewAnchor.js:1-49`, `lib/shaders/inversiveSampler.glsl.mjs:127-176` `applyAnchor`). The anchor has a time budget of 6 ms.
- The pixel offset from the view centre is kept apart from `u_center` (`lib/shaders/viewOffset.glsl.mjs:1-13`).
- A comment notes that "world coordinates near 1 keep 6e-8" (`lib/shaders/overlay_editing.glsl.mjs:13-14`).
- A comment warns of "bad numerical problems when a reflection is close to, but not, across a line, i.e. across a very large sphere" (`lib/invlib/ComplexArithmetic.js:519`).

Casselman warns that for groups whose Tits cone is acute, separating two distinct chambers becomes harder and harder as a chamber $wC$ approaches the boundary of the cone in projective space ([CASSELMAN-2], section "Matrices", paraphrased). The behaviour of points on a wall or within `EPS` of it is undocumented (Section 13.1, item 2).

## 5. Forward Generation

### 5.1. Dunham's Layer and Exposure Algorithm

Dunham's algorithm draws the regular tessellation $\{p, q\}$ in layers ([DUNHAM07], Section 3, pp. 5-7):

- "The first layer is just the central p-gon... The k+1st layer consists of all the p-gons sharing an edge or vertex with a p-gon in the kth layer (and not in any previous layer)."
- A $p$-gon that shares an edge with the previous layer shares "p-3 edges with the next layer", the exposure `MIN_EXP`. A $p$-gon that shares only a vertex shares "p-2", the exposure `MAX_EXP`.
- A transformation is a matrix together with `orientation` and `pPosition`. The edge pairings are held in `edgeTran[]`.

This is the logic of the pseudocode, as the research transcribed it from [DUNHAM07], pp. 5-7:

```text
Replicate(motif): drawPgon(motif, IDENT)
  for i=1..p: qTran=edgeTran[i-1]
    for j=1..q-2: exposure=(j==1)?MIN_EXP:MAX_EXP
      recursiveRep(motif,qTran,2,exposure); qTran=addToTran(qTran,-1)
addToTran(tran,shift): if shift%p==0 return tran else return computeTran(tran,shift)
computeTran(tran,shift): newEdge=(tran.pPosition+tran.orientation*shift)%p
                         return tranMult(tran,edgeTran[newEdge])
recursiveRep(motif,initialTran,layer,exposure):
  DrawPgon(motif,initialTran)
  if layer<maxLayer:
    pShift=(exposure==MIN_EXP)?1:0; verticesToDo=(exposure==MIN_EXP)?p-3:p-2
    for i=1..verticesToDo:
      pTran=computeTran(initialTran,pShift); qSkip=(i==1)?-1:0
      qTran=addToTran(pTran,qSkip); pgonsToDo=(i==1)?q-3:q-2
      for j=1..pgonsToDo:
        newExposure=(i==1)?MIN_EXP:MAX_EXP
        recursiveRep(motif,qTran,layer+1,newExposure); qTran=addToTran(qTran,-1)
      pShift=(pShift+1)%p
```

These caveats apply:

- The pseudocode is "a simplified version that draws multiple copies for the cases p = 3 or q = 3". Those cases need different values of `pShift`, `verticesToDo`, and `qSkip` ([DUNHAM07], p. 7).
- It replicates the whole "p-gon pattern" rather than each copy of the motif, "which is more efficient" ([DUNHAM07], Section 3).
- Dunham's ISAMA 2007 paper generalizes the method to the tessellations `{p; q1 ... qp}` with arrays per vertex, and handles duplicates with a list of transformation matrices already drawn ([DUNHAM-ISAMA07], Section 3, partial notes only).
- Dunham builds the spanning tree from "a Hamiltonian path in the Cayley graph", and the recursion depth stays at "a few dozen", so round-off is not noticeable [DUNHAM03].
- Dunham states that "there is an error in the Pascal pseudocode" of the 1981 paper by Dunham, Lindgren, and Witte, and that "There is a correct algorithm given in the later paper Hyperbolic Symmetry" ([DUNHAM-ISAMA07], Section 3, "The General Replication Algorithm"). The 1981 paper is "Creating repeating hyperbolic patterns", Computer Graphics 15(3), pp. 215-223, which [DUNHAM86] lists as its reference 12 (p. 153).

The 1986 paper "Hyperbolic symmetry" gives the earlier form of the layer algorithm [DUNHAM86]:

- It defines the layers on p. 145 and prints the procedure `Replicate` as its Figure 9 (p. 146), with the driver for the later layers and the order of the matrix products on p. 147.
- It gives `Replicate` as "the algorithm for obtaining the entire repeating pattern from the p-gon pattern for the groups [p,q] and [p,q]+ when p > 3 and q > 3". It adds that "The algorithm must be modified slightly to handle the group [p+,q] or the cases p = 3 or q = 3", and it does not print that modification ([DUNHAM86], p. 145, transliterated).
- For $q = 3$ the $p$-gons "share either one or two edges with the previous layer", a case that is "not considered in this discussion" ([DUNHAM86], p. 146).
- The only stopping parameter of the recursion is the layer count `nLayers` ([DUNHAM86], pp. 146-147).
- An isometry is a $3 \times 3$ Lorentz matrix acting on the upper sheet of the hyperboloid $x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = -1$, with $z > 0$ ([DUNHAM86], Section 6, p. 147).

hypertiling implements "two variants of the improved version of Dunham's combinatorial algorithm", as outlined in Dunham's 2007 paper "An algorithm to generate repeating hyperbolic patterns" and in unpublished notes of about 2007, and "The DUN07 kernel represents an almost literal Python implementation" ([HYPERTILING], Section 5.4, p. 28). Its `_replicate` "iterates over the polygon's vertices (self.p) and, for each vertex, over self.q - 2 polygons", in hyperboloid coordinates ([HYPERTILING-DUN07], secondary source only).

### 5.2. Automata-Based Enumeration

A word acceptor enumerates each group element once, as a word in the generators. These facts rest on secondary sources only:

- The Brink-Howlett automaton has as its states the subsets of the finite set $\Sigma$ of minimal roots. The transition on the generator $s_i$ is $S \mapsto \{s_i\} \cup \big((s_i(S) \cup \{s_i(\alpha_j) : j < i\}) \cap \Sigma\big)$ [ZHAO-BLOG].
- The automaton is built by breadth-first search and minimized with Hopcroft's algorithm. It accepts exactly the shortlex normal forms, one per element [ZHAO-BLOG].
- The drawing pipeline computes coset representatives of vertices, edges, and faces with integer arithmetic, applies the words to the base vertex in the linear representation of one higher dimension, projects, and draws. The blog claims that this avoids "issues with floating-point precision loss". Its example (7,2,3) has 30,517 vertices, 42,057 edges, and 11,541 polygons [ZHAO-BLOG].
- "the set of shortlex smallest representations is again a regular language", and its deterministic finite automaton "is essentially a GRTS that can be used to generate the tessellation". Outside simple cases "the Knuth-Bendix completion algorithm is not likely to terminate" ([CK21], Section 3).

Casselman's abstract states that "It seems to be good enough in many interesting cases to build the minimal root reflection table of Brink and Howlett" [CASSELMAN-EJC]. Section 11 of [CASSELMAN-A] covers the Brink-Howlett construction, which the research could not record in detail.

### 5.3. Regular Tree Structures

HyperRogue builds its graph combinatorially. Its heptagon graph "is built in an abstract way, i.e., not using the computations from hyperpoint.cpp", and its cells are obtained "by bitruncating the graph of heptagons" [HYPERROGUE].

Celinska-Kopczynska and Kopczynski generalize the HyperRogue method. They learn a regular tree structure in the style of Angluin, with edges labelled parent, left, right, or child state, and merge duplicates with a union-find structure that tracks orientation. They tested it on 149,629 tessellations ([CK21], secondary source only).

Thurn, Schrauth, and Erdmenger give a coordinate-free combinatorial layer method at "15.91 +- 0.74 ns per cell", "at least a factor of two" faster than Dunham's method. They note that for coordinate methods "an accumulation of numerical errors through repeated transformations can not be avoided" ([THURN], secondary source only).

### 5.4. Breadth-First Search With Deduplication

Breadth-first search (BFS) over tiles, with deduplication by coordinates, fails in floating point. These facts rest on secondary sources only:

- "Standard graph algorithms such as Breadth First Search are impractical because of the exponential expansion". The BFS variant failed in 58.45% of cases ([CK21], Section 3).
- The coordinate comparison uses thresholds on hyperbolic distance of 1e-2 for "different" and 1e-3 for "halt, precision issue". There were 586 precision errors, all in hyperbolic cases ([CK21], Section 3).
- hypertiling deduplicates by the sorted complex angle of the tile centres, "searching within the data structure in logarithmic time". It warns that "the binning mechanism always has a chance to accidentally miss duplicates" and that "the construction becomes unreliable and the user is duly warned" ([HYPERTILING], Sections 5.3.2 and 5.3.3).

SymmHub enumerates the tiles around a pattern box by folding a spiral grid of points and then walking across tile sides ([SYMMHUB], `lib/symhublib/CrownCalculator.js`). Its constants are `DEFAULT_MAX_COUNT = 1000`, `DEFAULT_MIN_SIZE = 1e-3`, and `epsilon = 1e-7` on the images of base points. The legacy version hashes the image of a registry point at an integer scale of 1e5.

The approaches differ in their guarantees. Zhao Liang relies on integer automata, Celinska-Kopczynska and Kopczynski rely on union-find and validation steps, and Dunham relies on shallow recursion to keep round-off small [ZHAO-BLOG] [CK21] [DUNHAM03].

### 5.5. Rasterizing Polygons Bounded by Circular Arcs

Geodesics in the Poincare disk are diameters and circular arcs orthogonal to the boundary. Geodesics in the upper half-plane are vertical lines and semicircles orthogonal to the boundary ([CFKP], Section 9, Theorem 9.3). Wikipedia writes a disk geodesic as $x^2 + y^2 + ax + by + 1 = 0$ or a diameter ([WP-POINCARE], secondary source only).

The documented methods for filling a polygon bounded by such arcs are these:

- A per-pixel inside test against each edge circle with the signed side test of Section 4.3 [SYMMHUB] [BULATOV18].
- Arc objects in a vector plotter. hypertiling notes that its routines "plot polygons with Euclidean straight lines instead of hyperbolic geodesics", which is "partially overcome by converting edges to arc objects" ([HYPERTILING], Section 3.6, secondary source only).
- Texture-mapped polygon meshes. EscherSketch uses "a non-affine texture mapping" and draws "around 50,000 hyperbolic polygons" "in 50 milliseconds or so" ([ESCHERSKETCH], secondary source only).

No source documents scanline filling of polygons bounded by circular arcs.

| Euclidean signed distance to a mirror circle | $\lvert z - c \rvert - r$ | [ZHAO-CP] `sdistanceToBall` |

No source read gives a closed form for the distance from a point of the disk to a geodesic that is not a diameter (Section 13.1, item 8). The distance from a point of the sphere to a great circle is in Section 9.4.

## 7. Antialiasing

### 7.1. The Scale Factor Tracked Through the Fold

Each conformal transform stretches lengths by the same factor in every direction at a point. Bulatov states: "Each transformation carries with it its stretching factor (transformations are conformal, therefore the stretching is uniform)" ([BULATOV14], p. 330).

SymmHub multiplies `scale *= r*r/|v-c|^2` on every inversion and leaves it unchanged on a reflection in a plane ([SYMMHUB], `lib/shaders/isplane.glsl.mjs:114-133`). It uses `pixelSize*scale` as the size of a pixel in the folded space, so a distance in the folded space divided by that product is a distance in screen pixels (Section 6.1). The model maps of Section 8.2 update the same factor before the fold. The step for a rational map uses `fwidth` instead ([SYMMHUB], `lib/shaders/projection_v2.glsl.mjs:154`).

Skia gives a form of the same idea that does not need GPU derivatives: "multiply a unit vector pointing along the SDF gradient direction by the Jacobian of the st coords... and take the length of the result", then `afwidth = SK_DistanceFieldAAFactor*length(grad)` and `smoothstep(-afwidth, afwidth, distance)`. It guards against the case where "the length of the gradient may be 0" ([SKIA], `GrDistanceFieldGeoProc.cpp`). Its application to a chain of folds is open (Section 13.1, item 19).

Quilez gives the distance estimate `float de = abs(v)/length(g);` and states "When analytical gradients can be computed, it is strongly recommended to use them instead" ([QUILEZ-DIST], secondary source only, read through a mirror).

### 7.2. Screen-Space Derivatives Across Folds

GPU derivatives such as `fwidth` are unreliable across fold seams. The Khronos extension states that "The method may assume that the function evaluated is continuous" and that "derivatives within the body of a non-uniform conditional are undefined" [KHRONOS]. A fold loop is non-uniform control flow, because neighbouring pixels take different numbers of folds. No tiling source names fold seams explicitly (Section 13.1, item 19).

Golus states that "fwidth() was never meant to be correct, just a fast approximation" and recommends `length(float2(ddx, ddy))` instead ([GOLUS], secondary source only). His gist gives two mitigations for seams, "Explicit Gradients" and "Least Worst Quad Derivatives" ([GOLUS-GIST], secondary source only).

### 7.3. Supersampling

| Implementation | Scheme | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nelson and Segerman | "for each pixel we sample 16 nearby points (in a 4x4 grid) and average the colour of all of the points which converged". They add that "antialiasing made a huge difference in the quality of small features" | [NS] Appendix A.1 |
| Zucker | 4 rook samples at offsets (0.125, 0.375), (-0.375, 0.125), (0.375, -0.125), and (-0.125, -0.375), for non-conformal projections only. Conformal projections take 1 sample | [ZUCKER] |
| SymmHub | None. Analytic antialiasing from one sample | [SYMMHUB] |
| knighty and Zhao Liang | The `AntiAlias` and `AntiAliasScale` presets of the Fragmentarium host | [KNIGHTY-DISK] [ZHAO-HH] |

Zucker also darkens a rim one pixel wide at the limit circle with `colorOut *= smoothstep(0.0, 1.0, abs(length(uv)-1.0)/px-0.5);` and writes `sqrt(colorOut)` as "approximate gamma correction" [ZUCKER].

### 7.4. Sub-Pixel Tiles Near the Limit Circle

Near the limit circle, tiles become smaller than a pixel. The published methods are these:

- SymmHub chooses the mipmap level of detail as `log2(TSIZE*u_pixelSize*scale*...)` from the tracked scale ([SYMMHUB], `lib/shaders/bufferToScreenImage.glsl.mjs:79-90`).
- SymmHub's palette antialiasing fades cosines by `smoothstep(6.28, 0.0, fwidth(x))` [SYMMHUB].
- Quilez fades a feature toward the mean of the signal "when the size of a 2 pi cycle is smaller than a pixel", replacing filtering "by band-limiting" ([QUILEZ-BAND], secondary source only). He notes that box filters flicker "under camera animation" and that a triangle kernel fixes this ([QUILEZ-CHECKER], secondary source only). The box filter footprint is `w = max(abs(dpdx), abs(dpdy))` ([QUILEZ-FILTER], secondary source only).
- Golus recommends to "clamp the size of the thing so it stays at least one pixel wide and then fade it out", and suggests fading to a solid colour ([GOLUS], secondary source only).

The poincare.sourceforge.net project notes that reverse pixel lookup "can generate visual artifacts due to rounding errors and aliasing problems" [POINCARE-SF]. Bulatov attributes the noise near the horizon to finite precision ([BULATOV18], secondary source only). Which mean colour to fade to, for example one weighted by area, is open (Section 13.1, item 20).

### 7.5. The sRGB Transfer Function and the PNG sRGB Chunk

This section gives the sRGB transfer function in both directions, the conversion between an 8-bit sample and the range 0 to 1, and the meaning of the PNG sRGB chunk, which declares that the samples of an image conform to the sRGB colour space [PNG3]. In the sRGB colour space of [CSS-COLOUR-4], 0% of a component is 0.0 and 100% is 1.0 (Section 10.2, secondary source only). No source read for this section states that samples are to be averaged in linear light (Section 13.1, item 24).

The standard that defines the transfer function is IEC 61966-2-1:1999 [IEC61966-2-1], with the Amendment 1 that [ICC-SRGB] cites. The research did not read the standard (Section 13.1, item 22), so the formulas below are as [CSS-COLOUR-4] quotes them.

Section 10.2 and the function `lin_sRGB` of Section 19 of [CSS-COLOUR-4] give the decoding direction (secondary source only):

$$C_{\mathrm{lin}} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{C'}{12.92} & \text{if } \lvert C' \rvert \le 0.04045 \\[1ex] \operatorname{sign}(C') \left( \dfrac{\lvert C' \rvert + 0.055}{1.055} \right)^{2.4} & \text{if } \lvert C' \rvert > 0.04045 \end{cases}$$

The sample code of Section 19 of [CSS-COLOUR-4] gives the encoding direction, in the function that converts linear light to gamma-encoded sRGB (secondary source only):

$$C' = \begin{cases} 12.92\, C_{\mathrm{lin}} & \text{if } \lvert C_{\mathrm{lin}} \rvert \le 0.0031308 \\[1ex] \operatorname{sign}(C_{\mathrm{lin}}) \left( 1.055 \lvert C_{\mathrm{lin}} \rvert^{1/2.4} - 0.055 \right) & \text{if } \lvert C_{\mathrm{lin}} \rvert > 0.0031308 \end{cases}$$

The sign and absolute value extend both directions to negative values by odd symmetry. The comments of Section 19 of [CSS-COLOUR-4] call this the "Extended transfer function" (secondary source only).

Other sources give the same constants:

- The ICC registry cites "Amendment 1 to IEC 61966-2-1:1999" and gives the encoding direction with the threshold 0.0031308 ([ICC-SRGB], secondary source only, read through a summarizing tool).
- The definition of relative luminance in [WCAG22] uses the threshold 0.04045 and $C' = I/255$ for an 8-bit sample $I$.
- Wikipedia gives both directions with the thresholds 0.04045 and 0.0031308 ([WP-SRGB], secondary source only).

The obsolete 1996 proposal ([SRGB96], Part 2) and the 1997 IEC committee draft ([IEC-CD1997], Sections 3.2 and 3.3) use the thresholds 0.03928 and 0.00304 instead, which are not the current values. The 1996 page states that "a small numerical error caused by rounding error was corrected" when IEC 61966-2-1 was standardized [SRGB96], and [WCAG22] states that its threshold was 0.03928 before May 2021. The slope 12.92, the offset 0.055, the scale 1.055, and the exponent 2.4 are the same in every source.

The 1997 draft limits RGB to the tristimulus values between 0.0 and 1.0 "by simple clipping" ([IEC-CD1997], Section 3.3). Whether the final standard keeps this rule was not checked (Section 13.1, item 22).

[SRGB96] and [IEC-CD1997] state the 8-bit conversion without a rounding step. Both give $C' = I/255$ and $I = 255\, C'$, with "a black digital count of 0 and a white digital count of 255" for 8 bits per channel ([SRGB96], Part 2, and [IEC-CD1997], equations (2) and (11)).

The PNG specification states the rounding, in its sections on decoders (secondary source only, read through a summarizing tool):

- Section 13.13 of [PNG2], "Decoder gamma handling", converts an integer sample $I$ of sample depth $b$ to the range 0 to 1 as $I / (2^b - 1)$, and a value $x$ in that range to a frame-buffer sample as $\lfloor x M + 0.5 \rfloor$, where $M$ is the largest frame-buffer sample, `MAX_FRAMEBUF_SAMPLE` in the source. [PNG3] has the same text.
- Section 13.12 of [PNG2], "Sample depth rescaling", rounds the same way. It rescales $I$ to $\lfloor I M_{\mathrm{out}} / (2^b - 1) + 0.5 \rfloor$, where $M_{\mathrm{out}}$ is the largest output sample, `MAXOUTSAMPLE` in the source.
- The code of Section 13.16 of [PNG2], "Alpha channel processing", divides an alpha sample by its maximum to decode it, and rounds with `(int) (gcvideo * fb_maxsample + 0.5)` to encode.

No source read states the rounding for an encoder that writes sRGB samples (Section 13.1, item 23).

Section 11.3.2.5 of [PNG3] defines the sRGB chunk (secondary source only, read through a summarizing tool). When the chunk is present, the image samples conform to the sRGB colour space, and a viewer should display them with the rendering intent that the chunk names. The chunk holds one byte, the rendering intent:

| Value | Name | Use that [PNG3] gives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Perceptual | "such as photographs" |
| 1 | Relative colorimetric | "such as logos" |
| 2 | Saturation | "such as charts and graphs" |
| 3 | Absolute colorimetric | "such as previews of images destined for a different output device (proofs)" |

[PNG3] recommends that "a PNG encoder that writes the sRGB chunk also write a gAMA chunk (and optionally a cHRM chunk) for compatibility with decoders that do not use the sRGB chunk", and states that "Only the following values shall be used":

- gAMA: 45455.
- cHRM: the white point $x = 31270$, $y = 32900$, red $x = 64000$, $y = 33000$, green $x = 30000$, $y = 60000$, and blue $x = 15000$, $y = 6000$.

A decoder ignores the sRGB chunk unless it is the colour chunk of highest precedence that the decoder understands. Table 1 of Section 4.3 of [PNG3] gives the precedence: cICP first, then iCCP, then sRGB, then cHRM and gAMA, and the chunk with the lowest priority number takes precedence. [PNG3] recommends that the sRGB and iCCP chunks do not appear together.

Section 11.3.3.5 of [PNG2] gives the same meaning, the same four intents, and the same gAMA and cHRM values. It adds that a colour-managing decoder should ignore the gAMA and cHRM chunks and use the sRGB chunk, and that a decoder without colour management uses the values above "as if they had appeared in gAMA and cHRM chunks" (secondary source only).

## 8. Other Models Per Pixel

### 8.1. Maps Between the Classical Models

Cannon, Floyd, Kenyon, and Parry give exact maps between five models: the upper half-space $H$, the disk $I$, the hemisphere $J$, the Klein model $K$, and the hyperboloid $L$ ([CFKP], Section 7, pp. 69-71):

| Map | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| $\alpha: J \to H$ | $(x_1, \dots, x_{n+1}) \mapsto \left(1, \dfrac{2x_2}{x_1 + 1}, \dots, \dfrac{2x_{n+1}}{x_1 + 1}\right)$ |
| $\beta: J \to I$ | $x \mapsto \left(\dfrac{x_1}{x_{n+1} + 1}, \dots, \dfrac{x_n}{x_{n+1} + 1}, 0\right)$ |
| $\gamma: K \to J$ | $(x, 1) \mapsto \left(x, \sqrt{1 - \lvert x \rvert^2}\right)$ |
| $\delta: L \to J$ | $x \mapsto \left(\dfrac{x_1}{x_{n+1}}, \dots, \dfrac{x_n}{x_{n+1}}, \dfrac{1}{x_{n+1}}\right)$ |
| Metric of $H$ | $ds_H^2 = \dfrac{\sum_{i \ge 2} dx_i^2}{x_{n+1}^2}$ |
| Metric of $J$ | $ds_J^2 = \dfrac{\sum dx_i^2}{x_{n+1}^2}$ |
| Metric of $K$ | $ds_K^2 = \dfrac{\sum dx_i^2}{1 - \lvert x \rvert^2} + \dfrac{(\sum x_i\,dx_i)^2}{(1 - \lvert x \rvert^2)^2}$ |

Kopczynski and Celinska-Kopczynska project a point $x$ of the hyperboloid, with $x_3$ the time component, to the Klein model as $P(x) = (x_1/x_3, x_2/x_3)$ and to the Poincare disk as $K(x) = (x_1/(x_3 + 1), x_2/(x_3 + 1))$. They obtain the upper half-plane from the disk "by applying a circle inversion", centred at the point $i$ ([KOPCZ24], Section 2, p. 3).

Wikipedia gives these maps in the plane (secondary source only):

| Map | Formula | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Disk to Klein | $s = \dfrac{2u}{1 + u \cdot u}$ | [WP-POINCARE] [WP-KLEIN] |
| Klein to disk | $u = \dfrac{s}{1 + \sqrt{1 - s \cdot s}}$ | [WP-POINCARE] [WP-KLEIN] |
| Hyperboloid to disk | $y_i = \dfrac{x_i}{1 + t}$ | [WP-POINCARE] |
| Disk to hyperboloid | $(t, x_i) = \dfrac{(1 + \sum y_i^2,\ 2y_i)}{1 - \sum y_i^2}$ | [WP-POINCARE] |

Section 2.3 gives the conventions for the map between the upper half-plane and the disk. The Gans model is described on Wikipedia in prose only, as "an orthographic projection of the hyperboloid model onto the xy-plane" ([WP-HYPERBOLIC], secondary source only).

### 8.3. The Band Model

SymmHub's band step is $z = \tanh(\pi w/4)$, and its code comment says that it "conformally maps infinite horizontal band -1 < y < 1 into unit disk" ([SYMMHUB], `lib/shaders/complex.glsl.mjs:77-82`). The version with a fraction $f$ reduces to it at $f = 1$ (lines 112-125). It agrees with the forward map $w = \frac{4}{\pi}\operatorname{artanh} z$ that Bulatov's page `math/1003` gives on the strip $\lvert \operatorname{Im} w \rvert < 1$ [BULATOV-RING]. The forward map in arXiv 2410.18420 is the same, with the metric $\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{\lvert dw \rvert}{\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}\operatorname{Im} w)}$ ([ARX2410], equation 1). Both are secondary.

No published source writes the inverse explicitly, so SymmHub's code and its comment are the only primary statement of it. Section 2.3 gives the conflicting coefficients.

Bulatov advises evaluating $\operatorname{artanh}$ in the complex plane as $\frac12(\log(1 + z) - \log(1 - z))$: "Proper way to calculate mapping by tanh^-1 on complex plane is to use linear combination of log()" ([BULATOV10], secondary source only).

### 8.4. Logarithm-Based Models

Bulatov gives these forward maps from the disk ([BULATOV10], secondary source only):

| Model | Map | Condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bent band | $w = \frac12\left(-a\log(1 - az) - \log(1 - z)\right)$ | $\lvert a \rvert = 1$ |
| Star | $w = \sum_i b_i \log(1 - a_i z)$ | Take care with the locations of the branch cuts, and choose the appropriate branch of the logarithm (paraphrased) |
| Ring | $w = \exp(z/a)$ | None stated |

Bulatov gives no inverse, series, Newton method, or code for these maps. A renderer needs the inverse, from the screen point $w$ back to the disk point $z$, and no source states it (Section 13.1, item 15).

### 8.5. Schwarz-Christoffel Maps Onto Polygons

A Schwarz-Christoffel map takes the disk conformally onto a polygon. For a regular $N$-gon it has this closed form ([REGPOLY], Section 3, equations 13 and 14):

$$f(z) = c_N \int_0^z (1 - \zeta^N)^{-2/N}\, d\zeta = c_N\, z\, {}_2F_1(a, b, c, z^N), \qquad a = \tfrac{2}{N},\ b = \tfrac{1}{N},\ c = 1 + \tfrac{1}{N}$$

Here ${}_2F_1(a, b, c, x)$ is the Gauss hypergeometric function with parameters $a$, $b$, and $c$. The constant $c_N$ is fixed by the area $\pi$, and its closed form was not recorded (Section 13.1, item 17).

For a general polygon, the Schwarz-Christoffel Toolbox evaluates the map per point ([SCTB], files under `@diskmap/`):

1. The derivative is $f'(z) = c\exp\left(\sum_k \beta_k \log(1 - z/z_k)\right)$ with $\beta_k = \alpha_k - 1$, where $z_k$ are the prevertices (`private/dderiv.m`).
2. To map a point, `dmap.m` finds the nearest prevertex and integrates from there by Gauss-Jacobi quadrature, with `scqdata(beta, 8)` and a tolerance of 1e-8. When the nearest prevertex is at infinity it starts from the conformal centre.
3. The inverse in `dinvmap.m` solves an ordinary differential equation with `ode113` "to obtain a fair approximation" and then improves it "with Newton iterations", with the update `dz = -F.*dF./(dF.^2-F.*ddF)`. Points that leave the disk are reflected back, and `evalinv.m` warns of a possibly large residual.

Trefethen and Driscoll state that the inverse "can be evaluated by a Newton iteration (the derivative f' is just the integrand...)" ([TD98], secondary source only). Trefethen notes that corners need special treatment, such as "compound Gauss-Jacobi quadrature", and that crowding scales as $e^{\pi L}$ [TREFETHEN25].

For a square, Cook gives the map from the square to the disk as $f(z) = \frac{1-i}{2}\operatorname{sd}\left(\frac{1+i}{2}Kz\right)$ and the inverse as $g(w) = (1-i) - \frac{1-i}{K}F\left(\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{1-i}{\sqrt2}w\right)\right)$, both with the elliptic parameter $m = \frac12$ ([COOK], secondary source only).

Kopczynski and Celinska-Kopczynska map the hyperbolic plane conformally to an arbitrary shape in the band model. They solve a discrete harmonic system in which "the position of each pixel inside is the average of the position of its" neighbours [NEWCONFORMIST], so the result is a lookup table per pixel, not a formula. They state that earlier approaches "are vulnerable to numerical precision issues or computationally expensive" [KC19]. The numerical details of the paper were not recorded.

What is available per pixel is therefore this. The regular $N$-gon has a forward closed form, a general polygon has a forward quadrature and an inverse by an ordinary differential equation followed by Newton steps, and an arbitrary shape has a precomputed table. No source gives a cheap per-pixel inverse for a general polygon.

## 9. The Sphere Per Pixel

### 9.1. Ray and Sphere Intersection

A ray $Q + t\,d$ meets a sphere with centre $C$ and radius $r$ where ([SHIRLEY], chapter "Adding a Sphere"):

$$t^2\, d \cdot d - 2t\, d \cdot (C - Q) + (C - Q) \cdot (C - Q) - r^2 = 0$$

With $a = d \cdot d$, $c = \lvert C - Q \rvert^2 - r^2$, and $h = d \cdot (C - Q)$, the near root in the half-b form is $t = (h - \sqrt{h^2 - ac})/a$ [SHIRLEY].

To avoid cancellation in the quadratic $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, Scratchapixel uses the stable form $q = -\frac12\left(b + \operatorname{sign}(b)\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}\right)$, with roots $x_1 = q/a$ and $x_2 = c/q$ ([SCRATCHAPIXEL], secondary source only).

### 9.2. Stereographic and Orthographic Alternatives

Stereographic projection is conformal, with $y_i = \frac{-2}{x_{n+1} - 1} x_i$ and the pulled-back metric $\frac{4}{(x_{n+1} - 1)^2}\sum dx_i^2$ ([CFKP], Theorem 8.1, p. 73). It maps spheres to spheres ([CFKP], Theorem 8.2).

The inverse projection from the plane to the unit sphere is ([WP-STEREO], secondary source only):

$$(x, y, z) = \left(\frac{2X}{1 + X^2 + Y^2}, \frac{2Y}{1 + X^2 + Y^2}, \frac{X^2 + Y^2 - 1}{X^2 + Y^2 + 1}\right)$$

SymmHub's sphere view treats the unit disk on the screen as a hemisphere, maps each point $p$ to $p/(1 + \sqrt{1 - \lvert p \rvert^2})$, and then folds in the plane (Section 8.2).

### 9.3. Folding in a Finite Group

Finite groups use a fold of fixed length:

- knighty's icosahedral fold is an unrolled sequence with no loop that depends on the data: `z=abs(z); t=dot(z,n1); if(t>0.0) z-=2.0*t*n1; t=dot(z,n2); if(t>0.0) z-=2.0*t*n2;`, repeated, and ending in `abs`. The normals are `n1=normalize(vec3(-Phi,Phi-1.0,1.0))` and `n2=normalize(vec3(1.0,-Phi,Phi+1.0))` [KNIGHTY-ICO].
- Syntopia states that "15 folds (3 generators x the max symmetry of 5) are required" for the domain of [3,5] ([SYNTOPIA], secondary source only).
- Stampfli inverts outside the arc circle, which gives a stereographic image of the sphere ([STAMPFLI], p. 71).
- SymmHub places spherical groups with the equator as the unit circle and uses fixed constants, for example `iSphere([-1.618...,0,0,1.902...])` for 532 [SYMMHUB].
- Sirgedas detects "which of the 120 'sectors' a point is in", takes absolute values so that "only 15 sectors remain", and applies the matrix of that sector ([SIRGEDAS], forum post).

### 9.4. Distance to a Great Circle

The Movable Type page gives the distance from a point to a great circle as $d = R(90^\circ - \alpha)$, where $\alpha$ is the angle between the point and the normal of the great circle ([MOVABLE], secondary source only, author documentation). The geodesy library computes `alpha = gc.angleTo(p) - pi/2` in `crossTrackDistanceTo` ([GEODESY], version 2.4.0, transliterated). The form $\sin d = \lvert x \cdot n \rvert$ on the unit sphere is not stated by any source (Section 13.1, item 10). Chapter 2 of the first edition of [RATCLIFFE] states no distance from a point to a great circle. Its nearest statement is the exercise $\sin a = \sin c \sin\alpha$ for a spherical right triangle with angles $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\pi/2$ and opposite sides $a$, $b$, and $c$ ([RATCLIFFE], first edition, Exercise 2.5.2(3), p. 53), which the book states without proof.

## 10. Pattern Fills

This section is a survey. Beyond flat colour, a tile can carry an image, a wave function, or a simulated pattern:

- SymmHub folds the point, maps it into a pattern box with a similarity and an optional Mobius "bend", and samples with linear, biquadratic, or box filtering, with a mipmap level of detail from the scale ([SYMMHUB], `lib/shaders/bufferToScreenImage.glsl.mjs:57-93`).
- SymmHub's crown mode composites copies from neighbouring cells, so that an image can overhang $F$. Its image arrays are selected per colour permutation [SYMMHUB].
- The flip parity of Nelson and Zucker selects a mirrored texture lookup [NELSON-CODE] [ZUCKER]. EscherSketch maps textures onto mesh triangles non-affinely ([ESCHERSKETCH], secondary source only).
- Von Gagern and Richter-Gebert deform a Euclidean ornament conformally into a hyperbolic one and draw it with the reverse pixel lookup of Section 4.1 [VGRG]. They tried the hypergeometric map onto a triangle and found it impractical, with unstable numerics from "crowding effects" and about one hour of CPU time, so they use discrete conformal maps instead ([VGRG], Sections 3.1 and 3.3, pp. 10-19).
- Stampfli maps a rotational rosette as $(r, \varphi) \mapsto (r^p, k\varphi)$, suggesting $p = 2$ ([STAMPFLI], p. 73).
- SymmHub runs a reaction-diffusion simulation of the `Gray-Scott` model on a square texture and symmetrizes it with a periodic pass of fold and resample. Its `useHMetric` option scales diffusion by $(1 - r^2)^2$ [SYMMHUB].

Farris's wave functions sum plane waves averaged over the group. These facts rest on third-party code and are secondary:

- The Bowdoin code writes a term with p4 symmetry as `(ei(N*X+M*Y)+ei(-M*X+N*Y)+ei(-N*X-M*Y)+ei(M*X-N*Y))/4.0`, and the pattern is a weighted sum of such terms. Its colour wheel looks up a photograph over $z \in [-2, 2)^2$, with an `overflowColor` outside [BOWDOIN].
- Rosenbluth's Haskell package cites "Appendices A and B" of Farris and writes `enm n m x y = exp(2*pi*(n*x+m*y) .*^ im)`, with the term of 3-fold symmetry `wnm = (1/3)(enm n m + enm m (-n-m) + enm (-n-m) n)` [WALLPAPER-HS].
- A GLSL port implements "the Hexagonal lattice recipe P6 from Farris p213" [PADDY].
- Farris states that "The complex value of the wave function at any given point is enough to locate a pixel in the photograph", and that the method extends to "certain non-Euclidean symmetries in the Poincare Upper Halfplane" [SCIAM].
- The term "domain coloring" was "coined by Frank Farris, possibly around 1998" [WP-DOMAIN].

Farris's recipes for the sphere and the hyperbolic plane were not found (Section 13.1, item 21).

## 11. CPU Performance

Pixels are independent in per-pixel pull-back (Section 3), and Syntopia says that the method parallelizes trivially on GPUs ([SYNTOPIA], secondary source only, paraphrased). Most pixels need few folds, so the cap barely affects the run time ([STAMPFLI], p. 70).

The number of folds varies between neighbouring pixels, and that variation costs SIMD (single instruction, multiple data) efficiency. The ISPC guide states that "control flow divergence leads to reductions in SIMD efficiency (and thus performance)" and offers the coherence hints `cif`, `cfor`, and `cwhile` [ISPC]. Rayon schedules uneven work by work stealing [RAYON-JOIN], and `with_min_len` and `with_max_len` control the granularity [RAYON-ITER].

No published CPU benchmark of a tiling renderer was found. The nearest analogue is a Rust Mandelbrot renderer at 3200 by 3200 pixels on an i7-12700K, with a masked-lane loop that breaks on `undiverged_mask.any()` ([PYSPEED], secondary source only):

| Variant | One core | All cores, rayon `par_chunks_mut` | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Scalar | 617 ms | 45 ms | [PYSPEED] |
| `std::simd` | 135 ms | 17 ms | [PYSPEED] |
| `wide` crate on stable Rust | 223 ms | 19 ms | [PYSPEED-WIDE] |

The published timings of forward generation are 15.91 ns per cell ([THURN], secondary source only) and about 50,000 polygons in about 50 ms ([ESCHERSKETCH], secondary source only). Dunham replicates the whole pattern of a $p$-gon rather than each motif, which is "more efficient" [DUNHAM07]. SymmHub repaints on demand [SYMMHUB]. No source gives a mean fold count per pixel for a hyperbolic group.

## 13. Open Questions and Unverified Claims

### 13.1. Open Questions and Unverified Claims

The items marked "candidate" were derived by a researcher and found in no source. Each needs a published source or a machine-checked proof before the body can state it as a fact.

1. Exact step count. Candidate: for a point in the interior of a chamber, reflecting across any violated wall of $C$ removes exactly one separating hyperplane, so the fold ends in exactly $\ell(w)$ steps whatever violated wall is chosen. Davis narrows this but does not settle it. He states that length equals the number of separating walls for group elements in the Cayley graph (Lemma 4.2.1) and that reflecting shortens an element exactly when its wall separates (Lemma 4.2.2), with a drop of exactly one only for fundamental reflections. He states the chamber form for the Tits cone (Lemma D.2.2), but nothing for a point of a hyperbolic polytope group, and no bound on the number of steps (Section 4.7). [VGRG] gives no bound either (Section 4.1), and the first edition of [RATCLIFFE], [BEARDON], and [EPSTEIN-PETRONIO1994] state neither termination nor a step count (Section 4.7). The left and right convention ($wC$ against $w^{-1}C$) also needs settling. [HUMPHREYS] Sections 1.6 and 1.7 may settle it.
2. Points on a wall or within `EPS` of one. The risks are alternation between two nearly coincident tests and ambiguity about the chamber. SymmHub uses `EPS = 1e-7` with the snap to zero commented out (Section 4.1). Davis gives the stabilizer of a point on a wall as a parabolic subgroup ([DAVIS], p. 68), and [VGRG] neglects "pixels on boundaries", but no source documents the numerical behaviour beyond Casselman's remark in Section 4.8.
3. Ideal vertices. The polygon theorem for domains with ideal vertices has primary sources (Section 4.2). How the fold behaves near an ideal vertex, where two sides meet at the limit circle, needs a source. The first edition of [RATCLIFFE], [BEARDON], and [EPSTEIN-PETRONIO1994] do not address it.
4. Order of the sides in the fold loop. Nelson's code comments suggest that the order matters for minimal flip counts (Section 4.4). [VGRG] does not state the order in which violated boundary lines are tested. No source states the effect.
5. Growth of the fold count. Candidate: the fold count grows as $\Theta\left(\ln\frac{1}{1 - \lvert z \rvert}\right)$, with a constant that depends on the group. The ingredients are [CFKP] Fact 5, Stampfli's "one unit cell" per step, and the Svarc-Milnor lemma ([WP-SVARC], secondary source only), which states that the orbit map "is a quasi-isometry". Davis gives growth by word length only, and exponential growth of a group that is not of Euclidean type follows from his Proposition 17.2.1 without being stated in that form. He gives no count of chambers within a hyperbolic distance (Section 4.7).
6. Kopczynski's exponents. The reading of $1 - \Theta(\exp(2d))$ and $1 - \Theta(\exp(d))$ as magnitudes $e^{-2d}$ and $e^{-d}$ (Section 2.3) is the research's, not the source's.
7. Termination of side-pairing folds. Whether a fold by a Fuchsian side pairing terminates without a convex domain, and whether Cheritat's Dirichlet-domain reduction, stated for $\mathbb{H}^3$, applies to Fuchsian side pairings and how to choose the base point $M$. SymmHub assumes a convex domain (`lib/invlib/Inversive.js:1249`). Davis proves the Dirichlet property only for finite linear reflection groups (Complement 6.6.10), and [VGRG] states its loop for a general "central tile" without a termination argument. The first edition of [RATCLIFFE], [BEARDON], and [EPSTEIN-PETRONIO1994] were searched without result (Section 4.7).
8. Distance from a point of the disk to a geodesic circle that is not a diameter. Candidate: $\sinh d = \dfrac{\big\lvert \lvert z - c \rvert^2 - r^2 \big\rvert}{r\,(1 - \lvert z \rvert^2)}$ for the geodesic on the circle with centre $c$ and radius $r$. It agrees with a numerical minimization in the cases that two readers tried, which is not a source. The hyperboloid form, the half-plane forms, and the form for a diameter of the disk are stated (Section 6.2), and no source read states this disk form.
9. Centre and radius of a disk geodesic. Candidate: the circle $x^2 + y^2 + ax + by + 1 = 0$ has centre $(-a/2, -b/2)$ and radius $\sqrt{a^2/4 + b^2/4 - 1}$, by completing the square. Ratcliffe states that a sphere $S(a, r)$ orthogonal to the boundary sphere has $r^2 = \lvert a \rvert^2 - 1$ ([RATCLIFFE], first edition, Section 4.5, p. 129), and Beardon states the same condition as $\lvert a \rvert^2 = 1 + r^2$ ([BEARDON], p. 128). Neither states the coefficient form above.
10. Distance from a point of the sphere to a great circle. Candidate: $\sin d = \lvert x \cdot n \rvert$ for unit vectors $x$ and $n$. It follows from the Movable Type formula of Section 9.4 but is not stated in that form. Chapter 2 of the first edition of [RATCLIFFE] was searched without result (Section 9.4).
11. Orthographic view of the sphere. The relation $z = \sqrt{1 - x^2 - y^2}$ is not named by any source, although SymmHub's sphere view uses it in code (Section 8.2).
12. Scale factor of SymmHub's sphere view. The code multiplies the scale by the single factor $1/\zeta$ with $\zeta = \sqrt{1 - \lvert p \rvert^2}$ (Section 8.2). The view is not conformal, so whether one scalar is the right factor for borders and antialiasing is open. Candidate: a radial factor $1/(\zeta(1 + \zeta))$ and a tangential factor $1/(1 + \zeta)$, derived by a researcher and stated nowhere in the code.
13. SymmHub's map from the upper half-plane to the disk. Candidate: `uhp2disc` is $w = \frac{i - z}{i + z}$, the Cayley map $\frac{z - i}{z + i}$ rotated by $\pi$, and its scale factor $2/(\lvert z \rvert^2 + 2y + 1)$ equals $\lvert dw/dz \rvert = 2/\lvert z + i \rvert^2$. A researcher derived this by algebra from the code in Section 8.2.
14. Inverse of the band map. $z = \tanh(\pi w/4)$ is stated only in SymmHub's code and its comment (Section 8.3), not in a published source. The first pass of the research derived $z = \tanh(\pi w/2)$ for Bulatov's $\frac{2}{\pi}$ scaling, which the second pass treats as a typo (Section 2.3).
15. Inverses of the ring, bent-band, and star maps of Section 8.4, with the handling of the branch cuts of the logarithm.
16. Gans and hemisphere coordinate maps in the plane, beyond the maps of [CFKP] Section 7. The research derived them only.
17. Regular $N$-gon Schwarz-Christoffel map. The constant $c_N$ is not recorded. Candidate: the power series $z\sum_k \frac{(2/N)_k}{k!\,(Nk + 1)} z^{Nk}$, whose slow convergence near the prevertices no source quantifies. An inverse suitable per pixel is also missing.
18. Star polygons. Candidate: a general Schwarz-Christoffel disk map with alternating $\beta_k$ handles reflex vertices. No closed form was found.
19. Antialiasing through the fold. SymmHub tracks the scale in code (Section 7.1), but no paper states the composite width through a chain of folds. Applying Skia's Jacobian method to such a chain, and the Khronos statement on derivatives to fold seams, are inferences of the research.
20. Mean colour for fading sub-pixel tiles, for example weighted by the area that each colour covers in $F$. Only the generic method of Golus and Quilez is cited.
21. Farris's recipes for the sphere and the hyperbolic plane. Sources that may give them are [FARRIS15] and [FARRIS01].
22. The text of IEC 61966-2-1:1999 and its Amendment 1 of 2003. The research did not read [IEC61966-2-1], so the current constants of Section 7.5 rest on [CSS-COLOUR-4] and [ICC-SRGB], read through a summarizing tool, on [WCAG22] for the threshold 0.04045 only, and on [WP-SRGB]. Whether the standard states an 8-bit quantization or rounding rule, and whether it keeps the clipping to the range 0.0 to 1.0 of [IEC-CD1997], is also open. Reading the standard closes this item, and so may reading [ICC-SRGB-PDF], which could not be decoded, with a local PDF tool.
23. Rounding when encoding. [PNG2] rounds to the nearest integer with $\lfloor x M + 0.5 \rfloor$ only in its sections on decoders and on sample depth rescaling, and [SRGB96] and [IEC-CD1997] give $I = 255\, C'$ with no rounding step. Applying $\lfloor 255\, C' + 0.5 \rfloor$ in an encoder, and clamping $C'$ to the range 0 to 1 before it, are inferences of the research. A source that states the quantization of an sRGB encoder, such as [IEC61966-2-1], closes this item.
24. Averaging in linear light. No source read for Section 7.5 states that the samples of a supersampled pixel, or the coverage of an antialiased border, are to be averaged in linear light rather than in encoded values. Zucker's `sqrt(colorOut)` as "approximate gamma correction" (Section 7.3) is the only related step in the other sources of this document. A source on compositing or on antialiasing that states the rule closes this item.
25. Raw text of the specifications. The statements of Section 7.5 from [CSS-COLOUR-4], [PNG2], [PNG3], and [ICC-SRGB] were read through a summarizing fetch tool, not as raw text, and carry the mark "(secondary source only)". The section number of the decoder gamma handling in [PNG3] was not checked against its table of contents. Reading the raw HTML of the cited sections removes the mark.
26. Distance from a point of the hyperboloid to a geodesic, with the time coordinate last. Candidate: $\operatorname{arsinh}\left(\lvert \langle x, u \rangle \rvert / \sqrt{-\langle x, x \rangle}\right)$ for a unit spacelike normal $u$ and a time-like vector $x$ of any length. [RATCLIFFE], first edition, equation (3.2.8), states the form with the time coordinate first, and no source read defines the norm for a vector of any length, so the design records this reading under `UNV-14` of `docs/design/geometry.md`.
27. Distance in the Euclidean plane from a point to a line with a unit normal. Candidate: $\lvert c \cdot x \rvert$, the absolute value of the line covector $c$ applied to the point $x$ in homogeneous coordinates with last coordinate 1. No source read states it, so the geometry design records it as a statement of its own, which a test pins (Section 12 of `docs/design/geometry.md`).
